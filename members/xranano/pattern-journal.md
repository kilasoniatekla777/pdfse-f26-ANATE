Tekla and I recognized the booking problem while talking about how tiring the manual process was for us as clients. We were familiar with contacting providers and waiting for information, so our starting point came from our own experience. My [problem-pool entries](../../00-foundation/problem-pool.md) also describe Nika arranging a birthday dinner, Tatuli searching for an apartment, and Elene finding a doctor. These gave us other concrete problems to compare rather than committing to an idea without alternatives.

Although my primary accountability was build and delivery in the [team contract](../../00-foundation/team-contract.md), I also helped investigate the problem. ## Week 2 · Evidence over opinion

I asked the questions in [Maya's interview (01)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/01-nail-customer-tbilisi.md), [Leila's (02)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/02-nail-customer-tbilisi.md), and [Nina's (03)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/03-nail-customer-tbilisi.md). Looking across their answers, I can see why our own frustration was only a starting point. Maya needed to know whether removal fitted into the appointment; Leila needed confidence in the particular artist; Nina needed an actual opening inside a limited afternoon. A booking platform would have to address the information each customer needed, rather than assume that showing times alone solved the problem.

I also noticed how easily I could steer a conversation toward our idea. In a practice interview with my mom, I initially asked whether online booking was a problem in Tbilisi and how comfortable she would be with it. I changed to “At which point, if any, did it become difficult? What happened next?” The [practice reflection](practice-reflection.md) records that correction; its date is unknown, so I do not claim it happened specifically in Week 2. It gave me a way to improve the questions before treating positive opinions as evidence.

Conducting interviews took me beyond my build role. Codex organized the supplied answers into the published records, as disclosed in the [AI usage log](../../docs/ai-usage-log.md). We formed the hypothesis before interviewing according to our [team clarification](../../01-discovery/problem-hypothesis.md), but first committed it afterward; the repository cannot independently establish the earlier wording and timing.

## Week 3 · Kill your favorite idea

Our initial idea assumed a gap between customers and salons: manual contacting was tiring, and a platform could connect the two more directly. I still see value in investigating that gap, but the interviews challenged the stronger claim that waiting for availability was the main problem for everyone. I logged [Sofia (04)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/04-nail-customer-tbilisi.md), [Elena (05)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/05-nail-customer-tbilisi.md), and [Amara (06)](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/06-nail-customer-tbilisi.md) while Tekla asked. Their difficulties were trusting a new salon, a conflicting reminder, and unclear extras plus group timing. In the [first-six-interview analysis](../../01-discovery/synthesis/patterns-analysis.md), only Nina clearly raised finding an opening as the main difficulty.

The trust and continuity evidence also challenges the assumption that customers will readily switch to whichever salon has a free time. Elena preferred her usual technician over a more convenient slot, and Sofia relied on a personal recommendation. That fits my observation that word of mouth matters in this area, but it does not measure the whole Tbilisi market. My takeaway is to keep the solution flexible: salon administration and customer needs differ, so we should investigate what can be automated and where personal communication still helps. That is a design question to test, not an established finding about every salon.

Keeping the counterexamples in the [affinity map](../../01-discovery/synthesis/affinity-map.md) and revising the questions in [script v2](../../01-discovery/interview-script-v2.md) gave us a smaller next step than building the entire marketplace. Codex prepared the written synthesis; my contribution was participating in the interviews and reconsidering the idea against those accounts.

## Week 4 · The prototype is the conversation

I helped with the interviews, the problem statement, and discussions about what to automate, what to leave flexible, and whether people would adopt the process. The [problem statement](../../01-discovery/problem-statement.md) focuses on knowing whether the complete service will finish before a later commitment. Tekla made the [commit](https://github.com/kilasoniatekla777/pdfse-f26-ANATE/commit/0db4aa6) and owns that Week 4 decision in [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md); my collaboration is recorded in my [personal work record](personal-work-record.md).

Tekla and I also built a simple frontend and showed it mostly to friends and family. A frontend gave us something concrete to discuss without implementing the whole system. Their feedback was positive, and afterward they sometimes pointed out situations where they thought it would be useful. That encouraged us, but it does not yet show that someone can complete a booking or that salons will adopt it. The frontend link/source, demonstration dates, and detailed feedback have not been added to this repo; this is my retrospective account of that work.

Guja's newer interviews add a useful contrast: [07](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/07-nail-customer-tbilisi.md) describes a difficult short-notice booking, while [08](../../01-discovery/interview-logs/08-nail-customer-tbilisi.md) describes an easy WhatsApp booking with the same technician, more notice, and a clear finish time. The contrast suggests that urgency and the information supplied matter; two accounts cannot establish the cause. My next step is to use the frontend for an observed booking task and record what people actually do, including whether they still want to call the salon. That task test is a next step, not a completed activity.
